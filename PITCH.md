# Fair Split — Product Manager Pitch

Three versions: 30-second elevator pitch, 2-minute investor/team pitch, and full narrative with supporting reasoning. Plus a slide-by-slide deck outline and Q&A armor.

---

## 1. The 30-Second Elevator Pitch

> "Roommates split rent, but everything else — groceries, internet bills, whose turn it is to clean the bathroom — lives in group chats and sticky notes, and it quietly breeds resentment. Fair Split is the first app that treats expenses AND chores as one fairness ledger. You open it, and one glance tells you if you're square with your household this week: 'You owe $45 and 50 chore points.' No Splitwise plus a paper chore wheel. No mental math. One number, one app, for the messiest part of sharing a home."

## 2. The 2-Minute Pitch

> "Every roommate knows this conversation: 'I paid for the groceries.' 'Well, I cleaned the whole kitchen.' Nobody's lying, and nobody can settle it, because there's no single place where money and effort live together. Splitwise counts dollars. A chore wheel counts turns. Fair Split is the first product that combines both into one fairness balance per person.
>
> Here's how it works. A household is created in seconds with a shareable invite code. Expenses split themselves — enter $90 of groceries and it's $45 each, with rounding handled so the ledger never has a phantom cent. Chores carry point values, so cleaning the bathroom counts for more than taking out the trash. And a dashboard computes your combined balance: the money side minus your share of others' expenses, plus your chore credit against your fair share. Cash settlements are logged so the balance reflects real life.
>
> Who is it for? The 75+ million people in the US sharing housing with non-family — students, young professionals, gig workers. These are people who already use Splitwise for money but still argue about chores, because nobody's built the bridge.
>
> We've built the MVP: accounts, households with invite codes, expense splitting, weighted chores with a leaderboard, settlements, and the combined fairness balance — demoed end to end today.
>
> The moat isn't the feature set; it's the single combined fairness number, and the behavioral loop: every expense and every chore updates one number everyone can see, so the data stays fresh because it's social, not mandatory.
>
> Next: filtered history, weekly email summaries, auto-rotating chore schedules, and receipt photos — then a freemium model where households pay for advanced analytics and cross-household support.
>
> I'm looking for [funding / pilot households / team support] to take this from a working MVP to a product that makes 10,000 households stop arguing before the end of the year."

## 3. Full Pitch Narrative (for slides or a longer talk)

### 3.1 Hook — the scene
Paint the moment: Friday night, two roommates, a fridge full of groceries one person paid for, a sink full of dishes one person cleaned. The question — "who's ahead this week?" — has no answer, because there's no shared ledger where money and effort mean the same thing. That question is the product.

### 3.2 The problem
- Rent is split cleanly; everything else is chaos: group chats, sticky notes, "I'll get you back later."
- People track money in Splitwise and chores on paper — two systems, zero reconciliation.
- The real cost is relational: unresolved small debts and invisible labor quietly breed resentment, the #1 reason shared living arrangements break down.
- Asymmetric visibility: whoever pays up front feels taken advantage of; whoever cleans feels uncredited.

### 3.3 The insight
Unpaid labor can be made comparable to money — not equal, but comparable — by giving chores a point value and converting "fair share" math into one balance. Nobody has shipped this. Chore apps exist (Sweepy, Tody); money apps exist (Splitwise, Settle Up). None combines them, and that combination is what makes the fairness question answerable.

### 3.4 The product (what exists today — all demoable)
- **Households + invite codes**: create in seconds, join with an 8-character code, one household per person (MVP simplification).
- **Expense splitting**: payer, amount, split-with selection, exact-share math with rounding handled.
- **Weighted chores**: point values, one-click completion, per-member leaderboard.
- **Settlements**: log cash paid between members; balances update.
- **The Fairness Balance**: one glance tells you if you're square — money line + chore line, each green (ahead) or red (owe), with a full per-person breakdown underneath.
- **Admin controls**: invite code rotation, member removal.

### 3.5 Why now
- Cost of living pressure is pushing more people into shared housing than ever — roommate demand keeps rising in metros.
- Splitwise normalized "splitting apps" for a generation; the behavior is already trained, but the category has stayed money-only.
- Gen Z/Millennials are comfortable with quantified personal life (steps, streaks, budgets) — quantifying household fairness is the natural next step.

### 3.6 Market
- TAM: US shared-housing adults (estimate 75M+) — students, young professionals, co-living, gig workers.
- SAM: those already using a splitting app and actively arguing about chores.
- The wedge: one household = one admin who recruits roommates = compounding, word-of-mouth growth. Every household that works is a case study.

### 3.7 Business model (proposed)
- Freemium: core fairness ledger free; premium for filtered history, email weekly summaries, receipt photos, and household analytics.
- Later: cross-household support (a user in two households), landlord/co-living operator tier.
- Retention story: the app is sticky because the data is social — you can't quietly stop contributing to a shared ledger.

### 3.8 Metrics that matter
- Activation: household created within 24h of signup; invitee join rate (target >50%).
- Engagement: expenses + chore completions per household per week (the ledger only works if it's used ≥2×/week).
- Fairness health: % of households where everyone's balance is near zero each week (the "success" metric from the brief).
- Retention: weekly active households at 8 weeks.

### 3.9 Roadmap (honest — what's built vs. next)
- **Done (MVP)**: stories 1–9 of the brief — households, join codes, expenses with splits, chores + points, combined balance, settlements, admin controls.
- **Next**: edit/delete with grace period, filtered history, email summaries, chore auto-rotation, receipt photos, multi-household.

### 3.10 Risks & answers
- "People won't log expenses." → The number is shared and visible; social pressure replaces discipline (same reason Splitwise works). Habit-forming under-10-second flows.
- "Chore points are arbitrary." → Points are per-household configurable, and the balance is transparent — households tune their own weights.
- "Splitwise will copy it." → Category leaders have no incentive to add chore labor (it makes the money product more complex); a fast, focused startup owns the niche and the data moat of combined history.

### 3.11 The ask
Be specific: seed funding, pilot households, design/eng resources, or an incubator slot — whatever the audience can give. Tie it to a number: "10,000 households by end of year."

## 5. Q&A Armor (tough questions, short answers)

**"How is this different from Splitwise?"**
Splitwise answers "who owes whom money." It ignores labor entirely. We answer the bigger question — "who's ahead overall, including the dishes." The combined number is the product.

**"Why will people bother logging chores?"**
Same reason they log expenses in Splitwise: it's shared and visible, and the payoff is immediate — the balance moves. Completion is one tap.

**"What if a household disagrees on chore weights?"**
Weights are configurable per household and every number is transparent. The system is a mirror, not a referee — households tune it to their own values.

**"What's the moat?"**
Network effects per household (admin recruits roommates), plus the data moat: a combined money+chore history no other product has, which makes switching costs real.

**"Is one balance per person even fair? Two roommates aren't interchangeable."**
Fairness is a starting point, not a verdict: the per-person breakdown makes assumptions visible, and households can weight chores to reflect what they value.

**"Chores don't equal dollars."**
Correct — that's why we keep them as separate lines: money in dollars, chores in points, both green/red. The product makes the trade-off explicit rather than hidden inside an opaque score.
